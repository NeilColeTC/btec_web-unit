# Week 16 Slides – Distinction Evaluation & Professional Practice

---

## Slide 1 – Week 16

# Distinction Evaluation & Professional Practice

**Making the development journey visible**

---

## Slide 2 – Today's focus

We will examine:

- individual responsibility;
- creativity;
- effective self-management;
- decision making;
- evidence quality;
- final synthesis across design, development and review.

---

## Slide 3 – Retrieval

1. What makes evaluation different from description?
2. What is a trade-off?
3. Why keep failed-test evidence?
4. What is regression testing?
5. Why should an optimisation be linked to a requirement or user need?

---

## Slide 4 – The whole journey

A website project is not isolated tasks.

Think:

**brief → design → review → refine → build → test → debug → optimise → evaluate**

Your decisions connect these stages.

---

## Slide 5 – Professional-practice lens

The repository guidance identifies three important qualities:

- **individual responsibility**;
- **creativity**;
- **effective self-management**.

These should be visible across design, development and review.

---

## Slide 6 – Evidence, not adjectives

Weak:

> I was responsible and creative.

Stronger:

> Here is the decision I made, why I made it, what evidence records it, and what happened as a result.

---

## Slide 7 – Individual responsibility

Responsibility can involve:

- owning decisions;
- identifying problems;
- acting on evidence;
- debugging;
- prioritising;
- responding critically to feedback;
- recognising limitations;
- completing work to an agreed standard.

---

## Slide 8 – Responsibility is not “working alone”

Professional developers use:

- documentation;
- feedback;
- research;
- testing tools;
- colleagues/users.

Responsibility means owning the decisions and outcomes.

---

## Slide 9 – Feedback and responsibility

Responsible response to feedback is not:

> “Someone suggested it, so I changed it.”

Instead:

**feedback → evaluate → accept/adapt/reject → reason → action → evidence**

---

## Slide 10 – Creativity

Creativity is not automatically:

- unusual colours;
- animation;
- extra features;
- complex code.

Creativity is often purposeful problem solving.

---

## Slide 11 – Purposeful creativity

Examples include:

- adapting a technique to a new need;
- finding a clearer information structure;
- developing an original interaction;
- solving a constraint efficiently;
- comparing alternatives and selecting a proportionate response.

---

## Slide 12 – Creativity test

Ask:

1. What problem or opportunity existed?
2. What alternatives were possible?
3. What did I decide?
4. Why was the response suitable?
5. What evidence shows the outcome?

---

## Slide 13 – Complexity is not creativity

A simpler solution may be stronger if it is:

- easier to use;
- easier to maintain;
- more accessible;
- faster;
- more reliable;
- better matched to the client requirement.

---

## Slide 14 – Self-management

Self-management can be seen through:

- planning;
- milestones;
- prioritisation;
- versioning;
- file organisation;
- testing discipline;
- evidence capture;
- time management;
- scope control.

---

## Slide 15 – Completion is not enough

> “I finished on time.”

is weak evidence by itself.

Ask instead:

- What did you prioritise?
- What did you defer?
- How did you respond when plans changed?
- What records support this?

---

## Slide 16 – Evidence from the time

Useful contemporaneous evidence can include:

- design versions;
- review notes;
- decision logs;
- test records;
- defect records;
- before/after captures;
- code/version history;
- dated screenshots;
- project notes.

---

## Slide 17 – Git history

Version history can support a development narrative.

But:

> a commit is evidence of a change, not automatic proof of creativity, responsibility or criterion achievement.

Context and explanation matter.

---

## Slide 18 – Decision journey

Trace one important decision:

**requirement → initial idea → feedback/testing → decision → implementation → result → reflection**

---

## Slide 19 – Decision example

Generic example:

> Users struggled to locate event booking information.

Possible responses:

- change navigation label;
- add a prominent call to action;
- restructure the page;
- add another navigation layer.

Which is most proportionate depends on evidence.

---

## Slide 20 – Trade-offs matter

A strong decision often balances competing concerns:

- visual richness vs performance;
- compact UI vs immediate visibility;
- extra functionality vs reliability;
- custom behaviour vs native browser behaviour;
- ambition vs available time.

---

## Slide 21 – Evidence chain

For each important claim:

**claim → evidence → interpretation → consequence → judgement**

If one part is missing, strengthen it.

---

## Slide 22 – Weak retrospective story

Be cautious of:

> “I planned everything perfectly and had no problems.”

Real development normally involves uncertainty, revision and trade-offs.

Credible reflection acknowledges them.

---

## Slide 23 – Problems can be useful evidence

A genuine problem can demonstrate:

- diagnosis;
- persistence;
- prioritisation;
- independent reasoning;
- controlled refinement;
- learning.

Do not hide every mistake.

---

## Slide 24 – But do not manufacture problems

Never create a fake defect or invented difficulty for evidence.

Use the authentic development journey.

---

## Slide 25 – Self-management audit

Rate your process for:

- requirements tracking;
- planning;
- file organisation;
- version control;
- time management;
- regular testing;
- evidence capture;
- responding to setbacks.

Then justify the rating.

---

## Slide 26 – Evidence gaps

Three useful categories:

### Complete
Evidence clearly exists.

### Partial
Some evidence exists but explanation/context is weak.

### Missing
The evidence does not exist.

Do not invent missing evidence.

---

## Slide 27 – What can still be strengthened?

You may still be able to:

- organise existing evidence;
- explain existing evidence more clearly;
- label screenshots/test records;
- link artefacts to requirements;
- identify limitations honestly;
- complete legitimate final checks.

---

## Slide 28 – What should not be reconstructed falsely?

Avoid creating retrospective artefacts pretending they were produced earlier, such as:

- fake design-review feedback;
- invented test failures;
- false timestamps;
- fabricated user comments;
- fictional decision logs.

---

## Slide 29 – Synthesis

Strong distinction-level reflection connects process to product:

> Because I managed/tested/changed **X**, the resulting website **Y**…

not simply:

> I used good time management.

---

## Slide 30 – Process can have negative effects too

Example:

> Delaying responsive testing meant layout problems were discovered late, causing rework.

A critical reflection can be stronger than unsupported praise.

---

## Slide 31 – Professional language

Useful phrases:

- evidence demonstrates;
- I prioritised;
- I adapted/rejected this feedback because;
- the decision resulted in;
- a limitation was;
- this reduced/increased;
- next time I would;
- on balance.

---

## Slide 32 – Final evidence audit

Check coverage across:

- requirements and designs;
- review/refinement;
- implementation;
- original scripting;
- testing;
- optimisation;
- final evaluation;
- professional practice.

---

## Slide 33 – Evidence quality

Strong evidence is:

- relevant;
- specific;
- traceable;
- authentic;
- clearly explained;
- linked to the requirement or decision.

Quantity alone is not quality.

---

## Slide 34 – Submission coherence

Could another person follow the project journey?

Can they see:

**what was required → what you decided → what you built → what testing revealed → what changed → how successful it was?**

---

## Slide 35 – Assessment boundary

The Hot Wheels exemplar teaches techniques and process.

It should not provide:

- your Hot Beans decisions;
- your test outcomes;
- your optimisation story;
- your evaluation;
- claims about your individual responsibility.

---

## Slide 36 – Peer support boundary

A peer can ask:

- Where is your evidence?
- What decision did you make?
- Why did you reject that alternative?
- What changed as a result?

They should not write your reflective conclusions for you.

---

## Slide 37 – Final reflection structure

Use:

> Situation/requirement…
>
> My decision/action…
>
> Evidence…
>
> Result…
>
> What this shows about my practice…
>
> Limitation/lesson…
>
> Next time…

---

## Slide 38 – Final question

Which is stronger?

A. “I managed my time well.”

B. “When testing identified a critical form problem, I moved two cosmetic tasks below it in priority, fixed and retested the form first, then completed the remaining work after the core user journey passed.”

Why?
