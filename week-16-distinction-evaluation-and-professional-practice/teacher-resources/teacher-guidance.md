# Week 16 Teacher Guidance – Distinction Evaluation & Professional Practice

## Teaching intent

Week 16 is about **synthesis and evidence quality**, not teaching learners to write claims that they are creative, responsible or well organised.

The repository's assessment-boundary guidance states that BC.D3 concerns individual responsibility, creativity and effective self-management across design, development and review. It also warns that Git history can support discussion of development but is not automatic proof of authorship or criterion achievement.

Keep the classroom question focused on:

> **What did the learner actually decide/do, what evidence records it, and what effect did it have?**

---

## Three concepts

### Individual responsibility

Useful indicators may include:

- owning significant design/technical decisions;
- responding to problems without relying on a supplied assessed solution;
- making reasoned feedback decisions;
- identifying and recording defects honestly;
- prioritising work according to client/user needs;
- recognising limitations and managing corrective action.

Responsibility does not mean refusing help or working in isolation.

### Creativity

Avoid reducing creativity to visual novelty. Stronger evidence can come from:

- original problem solving;
- adapting a learned technique to a different requirement;
- comparing alternative solutions;
- combining techniques proportionately;
- designing original client-side behaviour;
- simplifying a solution to improve usability/reliability.

### Effective self-management

Potential evidence includes:

- milestone planning;
- requirement tracking;
- sensible prioritisation;
- scope control;
- file/code organisation;
- versioning/backups;
- regular testing;
- evidence capture;
- response to setbacks;
- completion of essential work before optional enhancements.

---

## Retrieval indicative answers

1. Evaluation makes a supported judgement about effectiveness.
2. Evidence makes claims traceable and defensible.
3. A trade-off is a compromise where a benefit brings a cost/disadvantage.
4. Repeating a relevant test after a change.
5. Checking whether a change broke previously working behaviour.
6. It shows the authentic problem → response → retest journey.
7. Any decision demonstrably linked to a genuine user/client need.
8. Critically deciding whether feedback should be used unchanged, modified or not used.
9. Design versions, review notes, commits/version history, defect logs, test records, before/after captures.
10. It shows a change but does not by itself establish who reasoned through it or whether a criterion is achieved.
11. Essential requirements are needed for fitness for purpose; enhancements are additional.
12. Simpler solutions may be more reliable, maintainable, accessible or proportionate.

Challenge: B is stronger because it shows prioritisation, requirement awareness and an effect on the development sequence.

## Safe modelling

Use a non-assessed scenario such as a college events website.

### Generic responsibility model

A mobile booking flow failed because a control was inaccessible by keyboard. The developer recorded the failure, prioritised it ahead of a decorative animation, considered replacing the custom control with a native element, implemented the simpler control and repeated keyboard tests.

Ask learners:

- Where is responsibility?
- Where is creativity/problem solving?
- Where is self-management?
- What evidence would make those conclusions defensible?

Do not translate the example into a Hot Beans solution.

---

## Evidence map conferencing

When a learner writes a claim such as:

> “I showed creativity when I made the website responsive.”

ask:

- What specific problem did you solve?
- What alternatives existed?
- What did you decide?
- Was the implementation your own response?
- What evidence shows the decision developing?
- What was the user/client consequence?

The goal is to improve reasoning, not provide a criterion statement.

---

## Evidence gaps

Teach learners to distinguish:

### Existing but poorly organised

Legitimate action: organise, label and explain it.

### Existing but weakly contextualised

Legitimate action: explain what the artefact shows and connect it to the project decision.

### Genuinely missing historical evidence

Do **not** encourage reconstruction that pretends to be contemporaneous. The learner may acknowledge the limitation and strengthen other authentic evidence where permitted.

---

## Common misconceptions

### “More commits = better self-management”

No. Commit frequency alone does not demonstrate quality of planning, decisions or outcomes.

### “Creative means lots of JavaScript”

No. Technique quantity is not creativity. Originality should remain purposeful and appropriate.

### “If I accepted all feedback, I was responsible”

Not necessarily. Mature practice involves evaluating feedback and sometimes adapting or rejecting it for defensible reasons.

### “A perfect project proves distinction”

Reflection on genuine difficulties, limitations and trade-offs can provide stronger evidence of professional practice than claiming nothing went wrong.

### “I need to invent something to fill every checklist row”

No. Checklists support evidence review; they do not justify fabricated evidence.

## Adaptive teaching

### Support

Use three prompts:

1. **What happened?**
2. **What did you decide/do?**
3. **What was the effect?**

Then locate one piece of evidence for each.

### Stretch

Ask learners to evaluate:

- whether an apparently successful decision caused another cost;
- whether a time-management choice improved or reduced product quality;
- whether feedback quality was strong enough to justify action;
- whether a more complex technical response was actually inferior;
- how their testing strategy shaped the final solution;
- whether evidence supports full or only partial claims.

---

## Final audit principle

A coherent submission should allow the assessor to follow:

**brief → learner design choices → review decisions → implementation → testing → defects/refinement → final evaluation → reflection on working practices**

More files do not automatically create better evidence. Prefer clear, relevant and traceable artefacts.

---

## Assessment-safe teacher prompts

- Show me where that decision is recorded.
- What did you personally decide?
- What evidence existed before the final evaluation?
- Why was that task prioritised?
- What alternative did you reject and why?
- What happened because of your approach?
- What would you manage differently next time?
- Does the evidence support the whole claim or only part of it?

Avoid phrases such as “write this for D3” or telling learners that an isolated action proves a criterion.

---

## Exit-ticket indicative answers

1. Ownership of decisions/actions/outcomes within the learner's own project.
2. Complexity can be unnecessary; creativity is purposeful problem solving and appropriate original decision making.
3. Examples: requirement tracker, plan, version history, test progression, defect log, prioritisation record, dated development evidence.
4. It can reveal diagnosis, prioritisation, persistence and evidence-led corrective action.
5. Version history shows changes, but assessment must consider context, authorship and the complete evidence.
6. Do not fabricate it; acknowledge the gap and organise/strengthen authentic evidence where possible.
7–8. Individual evidence-based responses.

## Preparation for Week 17

Week 17 should be a controlled finalisation/submission session: verification of requirements, links/files, final tests, evidence organisation, submission readiness and final reflective check. It should not introduce new assessed solutions or encourage late feature creep.
