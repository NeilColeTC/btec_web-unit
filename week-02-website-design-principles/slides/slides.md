## Slide 4 – What makes a website effective?

A useful judgement is not:

> “I like this website.”

A stronger judgement asks:

- Can users understand it?
- Can they find what they need?
- Can they complete key tasks?
- Is the content suitable for them?
- Can a wide range of users access it?
- Does the design support the site's purpose?

---

## Slide 5 – The analysis framework

For each feature, ask:

**Principle → Evidence → Effect → Audience → Purpose**

Example structure:

> The site uses **[principle]** through **[specific evidence]**. This helps users by **[effect]**. This is appropriate for **[audience]** because **[reason]**, supporting the site's purpose of **[purpose]**.

---

## Slide 6 – Usability

**Usability** is the extent to which users can use a website effectively, efficiently and with reasonable ease.

Look for:

- clear actions
- predictable controls
- readable information
- useful feedback
- sensible task flow
- minimal unnecessary effort

Question: Can users achieve what they came to do?

---

## Slide 7 – Site layout

Layout is the arrangement of elements on a page.

Effective layout can:

- establish visual hierarchy
- separate different types of information
- guide scanning
- highlight important content
- support different screen sizes

Ask: **What does the layout make the user notice first?**

---

## Slide 8 – White space

White space is the unused space between or around page elements. It does **not** have to be white.

It can:

- reduce visual overload
- group related content
- separate unrelated content
- improve readability
- draw attention to important elements

Too little can feel crowded; too much can hide relationships or waste space.

---

## Slide 9 – Spacing and alignment

**Spacing** controls the distance between elements.  
**Alignment** creates visual relationships between elements.

Good use can make pages:

- easier to scan
- more consistent
- more organised
- easier to understand

Poor alignment can make information appear unrelated or unfinished.

---

## Slide 10 – Navigation

Navigation helps users move through a website and locate content.

Examples include:

- navigation bars
- menus
- breadcrumbs
- internal links
- buttons
- search
- footer navigation

Strong navigation is usually clear, predictable and consistent.

---

## Slide 11 – Navigation question

Imagine you arrive on a website for the first time.

How quickly could you find:

- contact information?
- a specific product?
- a news article?
- account settings?

A navigation system should be judged by **user tasks**, not simply by appearance.

---

## Slide 12 – Typography

Typography includes choices such as:

- typeface
- size
- weight
- line spacing
- line length
- contrast
- hierarchy

Typography should support readability and communication.

Ask: Is it easy to distinguish headings, body text, labels and actions?

---

## Slide 13 – Clarity

Clarity means users can readily understand:

- what the page is about
- what choices are available
- what actions they can take
- what information is important

Clarity can be improved through headings, hierarchy, concise wording and predictable design.

---

## Slide 14 – Consistency and intuitiveness

Consistency means similar elements behave and appear in similar ways.

Examples:

- buttons use predictable styling
- navigation remains in a familiar position
- headings follow a clear hierarchy
- page structure uses recognisable patterns

Consistency helps interfaces become more intuitive.

---

## Slide 15 – Simplicity

Simplicity is not the same as having very little content.

A simple design removes unnecessary complexity while retaining what users need.

Ask:

- Is every element useful?
- Are choices understandable?
- Is important information easy to find?

---

## Slide 16 – Accessibility

Accessible websites consider users with different needs and ways of interacting.

Introductory considerations include:

- readable text
- sufficient contrast
- meaningful alternative text for images
- understandable link/button labels
- keyboard-friendly interaction
- clear structure
- content that does not depend only on colour

Accessibility is part of good design, not an optional extra.

---

## Slide 17 – Content and media

Content includes text, images, video, audio and other information.

Ask:

- Is it relevant?
- Is it accurate?
- Is it current?
- Is the amount suitable for the audience?
- Does the media support the message?
- Does media distract from the main task?

---

## Slide 18 – Accuracy

Accuracy applies to more than spelling.

Consider:

- factual accuracy
- prices and product information
- dates and opening times
- links
- labels
- contact details
- professional spelling and grammar

Inaccurate content can damage usability and trust.

---

## Slide 19 – One feature, several principles

A clearly labelled search box might contribute to:

- usability
- navigation
- clarity
- simplicity
- consistency

Design principles overlap. Strong analysis explains these relationships rather than treating them as isolated checklist items.

## Slide 22 – From description to comparison

**Weak:**

> Site A has a navigation menu. Site B also has a navigation menu.

**Stronger structure:**

> Both sites use navigation to support rapid access to key content, but they organise choices differently because users arrive with different goals. Site A prioritises…, whereas Site B prioritises… This supports…

The strength comes from **evidence + reason + comparison**.

---

## Slide 23 – Comparative language

Useful connectives:

- similarly
- in contrast
- whereas
- however
- both
- unlike
- more suitable because
- this is particularly important for
- this supports the audience by

Comparative language should connect ideas, not replace explanation.
