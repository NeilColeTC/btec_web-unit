# Week 8 Teacher Guidance – Design Review and Justification

## Curriculum purpose

Week 8 is the transition from design production into implementation. The session should ensure learners do not treat review as a superficial approval exercise. The important skill is evaluating feedback and using it to justify final design decisions.

The intended progression is:

**design → review → feedback → decision → refinement → justification**

This week is particularly important for building evidence toward **B.P3** and the reasoning required for **B.M2**.

## Key teaching messages

### 1. Review should be structured

Learners should present sufficient design evidence for another person to understand the proposed website. A review of one isolated wireframe is unlikely to produce enough useful feedback.

Encourage learners to include:

- requirements summary
- site map
- mood board
- main wireframes
- responsive alternatives
- navigation plan
- success criteria
- relevant technical notes

### 2. Feedback should be specific

Weak feedback includes comments such as:

- “Looks good.”
- “Nice colours.”
- “I like the menu.”

Intervene with questions such as:

- Which feature are you referring to?
- What user task does it affect?
- Why might this be a strength or weakness?
- Which requirement does it relate to?
- What change would you recommend?

### 3. Learners do not need to accept everything

A mature review process includes **accept, adapt and reject** decisions.

The learner's reasoning is more important than the quantity of changes made. A justified rejection can be stronger evidence than blindly implementing a suggestion.

### 4. Preserve evidence of iteration

Before/after evidence is essential. Learners should not simply overwrite their original wireframes or diagrams.

A useful evidence trail is:

1. original design
2. reviewer feedback
3. learner decision
4. revised design
5. explanation of improvement

## Suggested neutral modelling example

Use a fictional community-event website rather than Hot Beans Web.

Example review feedback:

> “The booking button is difficult to find because it appears below several blocks of promotional content.”

Model the reasoning:

- identify the relevant user task
- connect the issue to hierarchy/navigation
- decide whether to accept or adapt the feedback
- revise the wireframe
- explain why the new placement better supports the intended purpose

Avoid writing an equivalent Hot Beans Web response for learners.

## Indicative retrieval answers

1. A client requirement is something the client has explicitly requested or specified.
2. An inferred user need is a reasonable need identified from the audience/task even if not written directly in the brief.
3. A site map shows website structure and relationships between pages.
4. A mood board communicates intended visual style, tone, colour, typography and imagery direction.
5. A wireframe shows planned structure/layout rather than finished visual styling.
6. Responsive behaviour should be planned because content/navigation must remain usable on different screen sizes.
7. Pseudocode describes the logical steps of an interaction or process.
8. A flowchart visually represents process logic and decisions.
9. Asset sources/licences should be recorded to demonstrate lawful use and provenance.
10. A measurable success criterion can be tested or observed objectively.
11. A design constraint limits possible solutions, for example time, device, technical or legal restrictions.
12. Alternatives allow comparison before committing to one design approach.
13. Fitness for purpose means how well a solution performs the job it was intended to perform.
14. Feedback may be rejected if it conflicts with requirements, accessibility, usability, performance or another justified constraint.
15. **feedback/decision** depending on exact phrasing; the important sequence is review → feedback → decision → refinement.

## Review organisation

Aim for two identifiable reviews per learner. If class numbers permit, rotate reviewers so that the second reviewer is not simply repeating the first discussion.

Possible sequence:

- learner A reviews B
- learner B reviews C
- learner C reviews A

Then rotate again.

This can produce more independent feedback than reciprocal pairs alone.

## Evidence quality

For B.P3 preparation, look for:

- evidence that review actually happened
- identifiable reviewers
- specific comments
- proposed improvements
- decisions made after review
- visible refinement

For B.M2 preparation, look for:

- reasons linked to user needs
- reasons linked to purpose/client requirements
- comparison with alternatives
- consideration of constraints
- clear explanation of why the selected design is fit for purpose

## Common weak responses

### “I changed it because my reviewer told me to.”

Intervention: Why was the suggestion appropriate? Which user need or requirement does it improve?

### “Both reviewers liked the design so I kept it.”

Intervention: What evidence suggests the design works? Which specific features support the purpose?

### “I rejected it because I didn’t like it.”

Intervention: Replace personal preference with a design reason involving audience, requirement, usability, accessibility, performance or consistency.

### “I made it look better.”

Intervention: Identify the actual change and explain its functional or communication effect.

## Adaptive teaching

### Support

- pre-select five review questions
- use oral feedback before written recording
- provide accept/adapt/reject cards
- give learners a model neutral review record
- reduce the number of design decisions to justify initially
- prompt with: **feature → effect → user → requirement**

### Stretch

- require resolution of conflicting reviewer feedback
- ask learners to rank refinements by impact and feasibility
- require explanation of negative consequences/trade-offs
- ask learners to defend one rejected suggestion
- distinguish client preference from end-user need

## English, EDI and employability

**English:** professional discussion, listening, concise note-taking, explanation and justification.

**EDI:** encourage consideration of accessibility and different user perspectives. Avoid treating one peer reviewer as representative of every possible user.

**Employability:** relate the activity to design critiques, sprint reviews, client meetings, peer review, usability review and sign-off processes.

## Assessment integrity

This session occurs close to assessed design evidence, so tutor modelling should remain generic. Tutors can teach structures, questioning techniques and what good reasoning looks like, but should not generate learner-specific Hot Beans Web design decisions or review conclusions.

## End-of-week readiness

Before Week 9, learners should ideally have:

- complete or near-complete design documentation
- two structured review records
- feedback decision log
- revised design evidence
- justification of major final decisions

Week 9 can then begin implementation from a reviewed design baseline rather than from incomplete planning.
