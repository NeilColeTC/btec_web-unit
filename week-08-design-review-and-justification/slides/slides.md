# Week 8 Slides – Design Review and Justification

## Slide 1 – Week 8
**Design Review and Justification**  
B.P3 / B.M2 preparation

---

## Slide 2 – Today’s question
How do professional designers decide what to change before development begins?

---

## Slide 3 – Learning outcomes
By the end of today you should be able to:
- review a website design with others
- gather useful feedback
- decide what to accept, adapt or reject
- refine your design evidence
- justify final design decisions

---

## Slide 4 – Retrieval
What is the difference between:
- a requirement
- a design decision
- a constraint
- a success criterion
- an alternative design?

---

## Slide 5 – Why review before coding?
Changing a wireframe is quicker and cheaper than rebuilding a finished website.

Review can reveal:
- unclear navigation
- missing content
- poor hierarchy
- accessibility concerns
- weak responsive behaviour
- unnecessary complexity
- conflict with requirements

---

## Slide 6 – Review is not approval
A reviewer is not there simply to say whether they like the design.

Useful review asks:
**Does this design work for the intended user and purpose?**

---

## Slide 7 – Strong feedback
Useful feedback is:
- specific
- relevant
- evidence-based
- actionable
- connected to users, purpose or requirements

---

## Slide 8 – Weak feedback
Examples:
- “Looks good.”
- “I don’t like blue.”
- “Make it more modern.”
- “Add more pictures.”

What is missing from each comment?

---

## Slide 9 – Turn weak feedback into strong feedback
Weak: “The menu is confusing.”

Stronger: “The menu labels do not clearly show where job vacancies and the application form are located, so a first-time applicant may take longer to reach the main task.”

---

## Slide 10 – What should be reviewed?
Your review pack should include relevant evidence such as:
- requirements analysis
- site map
- mood board/style direction
- desktop wireframes
- mobile/tablet adaptations
- navigation plan
- technical notes
- success criteria

---

## Slide 11 – Review question types
Ask questions about:
- task completion
- navigation
- clarity
- hierarchy
- accessibility
- consistency
- responsive design
- content
- trust/credibility
- fitness for purpose

---

## Slide 12 – Better reviewer questions
Instead of “Do you like it?” ask:
- Can you tell where you would apply for a job?
- Which part of the page attracts your attention first?
- Is anything unclear or difficult to locate?
- Would this layout still work on a phone?
- Does the design feel suitable for the intended audience?

---

## Slide 13 – Two reviewers
Why use more than one reviewer?

Different people may:
- notice different problems
- have different priorities
- represent different user perspectives
- disagree

Disagreement is useful evidence if you evaluate it.

---

## Slide 14 – Record actual feedback
Do not replace feedback with a summary such as:
“Reviewer liked the site.”

Record what was said and what design element it referred to.

---

## Slide 15 – Feedback triage
For each suggestion choose:

**Accept** – implement as suggested  
**Adapt** – use the underlying idea but modify it  
**Reject** – do not implement

Every decision needs a reason.

---

## Slide 16 – Accept
Example:
A reviewer cannot find the application form because the navigation label is vague.

If clearer wording supports the main user task and client requirement, accepting the feedback may be justified.

---

## Slide 17 – Adapt
A reviewer asks for a large autoplay video on the homepage.

You agree that stronger media is needed, but choose a lightweight static hero image instead because of performance and accessibility concerns.

---

## Slide 18 – Reject
A reviewer wants animated backgrounds because they look exciting.

You may reject this if it reduces readability, accessibility, performance or professional suitability.

---

## Slide 19 – Evidence beats popularity
Two reviewers agreeing does not automatically make a suggestion correct.

A strong decision is based on:
- requirement
- audience need
- purpose
- usability
- accessibility
- performance
- constraint

---

## Slide 20 – Conflicting feedback
Reviewer 1: “Put every option in the main menu.”  
Reviewer 2: “The menu already feels crowded.”

Your job is not to vote.

Your job is to evaluate which approach best supports the design requirements.

---

## Slide 21 – Refinement
Refinement means improving a design while retaining its overall direction.

Possible refinements:
- clearer labels
- revised hierarchy
- improved spacing
- stronger contrast
- different content order
- simplified navigation
- responsive layout change
- altered form structure

---

## Slide 22 – Preserve before/after evidence
If you overwrite the original design, you lose evidence of development.

Keep:
1. original version
2. feedback
3. decision
4. revised version
5. explanation of improvement

---

## Slide 23 – From B.P3 to B.M2
B.P3 focuses on reviewing designs with others and identifying improvements.

B.M2 requires stronger reasoning about why design decisions meet user needs and are fit for purpose.

---

## Slide 24 – Weak justification
“I changed the navigation because my reviewer told me to.”

Why is this weak?

---

## Slide 25 – Stronger justification structure
**Decision → reason → user need → purpose**

Example structure:
“I changed ___ because ___. This helps the intended user by ___. It therefore supports the requirement/purpose to ___.”

---

## Slide 26 – Rejected ideas can strengthen evidence
A justified rejection can demonstrate strong design thinking.

Explain:
- what was suggested
- why it was considered
- why it was not suitable
- what evidence or requirement influenced the decision

---

## Slide 27 – Review activity
Reviewer 1:
- examine the design pack
- ask focused questions
- record specific feedback

Designer:
- explain decisions
- listen without defending every choice
- ask follow-up questions

Then swap roles.

---

## Slide 28 – Second review
Repeat with a different reviewer.

Look for:
- repeated issues
- conflicting opinions
- new concerns
- evidence of different user perspectives

---

## Slide 29 – Decision log
For every important feedback point record:
- feedback
- source
- accept/adapt/reject
- reason
- resulting change
- requirement/user link

---

## Slide 30 – Refinement workshop
Choose the highest-impact changes first.

Ask:
1. Does this solve a real problem?
2. Does it improve the intended user journey?
3. Does it preserve other important requirements?
4. Can I show clear before/after evidence?

---

## Slide 31 – Final evidence check
Do you have:
- two review records?
- meaningful reviewer comments?
- accept/adapt/reject decisions?
- reasons for decisions?
- before/after evidence?
- final design justification?

---

## Slide 32 – Exit ticket
Complete:

1. The most useful feedback I received was…
2. I decided to accept/adapt/reject it because…
3. The refinement improves the design for the user by…
4. One design decision I can now justify clearly is…
