# Week 6 Teacher Guidance – Visual Website Design and Prototyping

## Curriculum purpose

Week 6 translates the Week 5 requirements analysis into visible design evidence for Learning Aim B.

The key progression is:

**requirements → site structure → visual direction → page layout → responsive variation → annotation → justification**

Learners should leave the session with design evidence that is detailed enough to guide later development but still clearly their own work.

## Specification alignment

This week addresses B1 through:
- initial design ideas/prototypes
- diagrammatic design documentation such as site maps, mood boards and wireframes
- realistic representations at an appropriate stage
- consideration of mobile/tablet compatibility
- application of website-design principles

It primarily prepares B.P2 and also creates evidence that can later support B.M2 justification.

## Assessment boundary

The Hot Beans Web task is assessed work. Therefore:
- do not provide a complete Hot Beans site map
- do not provide a finished Hot Beans mood board
- do not provide finished page wireframes learners could reproduce
- use a neutral scenario for fully worked examples
- support learners through questioning, checklists and prompts
- allow learners to make and justify their own design decisions

## Indicative retrieval answers

1. **Client requirement:** something requested or required by the client brief.
2. **User requirement:** something the intended user needs from the website or interaction.
3. **Constraint:** a limitation or condition affecting the design/development.
4. A measurable criterion could specify that a named function works, a page is accessible through navigation, or a layout remains usable at a stated viewport/device context.
5. Traceability helps show that designs respond to requirements rather than arbitrary preference.
6. A site map shows page structure, hierarchy and relationships.
7. Information architecture is the organisation and structuring of information so users can find and understand it.
8. A mood board communicates visual direction and design language.
9. A wireframe communicates page structure, hierarchy and placement before visual polish.
10. A prototype is a representation used to explore or test how a design may look or behave.
11. Visual hierarchy controls the order in which users notice and interpret content.
12. Navigation, columns, image size/crop, spacing, content order and control layout may change responsively.
13. Mobile layouts require reprioritisation and reflow, not just reduction in size.
14. Description identifies what exists; justification explains why it was chosen and how it meets needs.
15. **requirement → design decision → intended effect**.

## Guidance for site maps

Push learners beyond drawing disconnected boxes.

Useful questions:
- Is every required content area represented?
- Which pages are top-level and why?
- Can a user predict where information will be?
- How many steps are required for key journeys?
- Is the structure consistent with the site's purpose?

## Guidance for mood boards

A weak mood board is a collage without rationale. Require learners to explain the intended influence of key references.

Prompt links to:
- audience
- tone
- professionalism
- trust
- accessibility
- readability
- visual hierarchy
- consistency

## Guidance for wireframes

Encourage low-fidelity work first. Learners often waste time selecting colours and images before resolving structure.

Check for:
- page purpose
- content hierarchy
- navigation
- calls to action
- required content
- forms where appropriate
- consistency
- responsive adaptation

## Responsive-design guidance

Do not accept “make everything smaller” as responsive thinking.

Ask:
- what stacks?
- what moves?
- what becomes more prominent?
- how does navigation change?
- are tap targets usable?
- does content order still make sense?

## Annotation quality

Weak annotation:
> “This is a button.”

Better reasoning structure:
> **Decision → requirement/user need → intended effect**

Prompt learners to connect decisions to usability, accessibility, audience, purpose and the brief.

## Common misconceptions and interventions

### “The mood board is just inspiration.”
Ask the learner to explain how each major reference could change the final site.

### “A wireframe needs final colours.”
Clarify that low-fidelity wireframes test structure and hierarchy before visual polish.

### “Responsive means mobile and desktop versions look identical.”
Explain that consistency of purpose and identity does not require identical layout.

### “My annotation says what everything is.”
Ask: Why is it there? Which requirement does it support? What effect should it have?

## Adaptive teaching

### Support
- provide a page-component bank
- allow hand drawing before digital design
- provide partially structured annotation prompts
- reduce initial wireframing to one key page
- pair learners for navigation-path testing

### Stretch
- require alternative navigation structures
- ask for mobile-first rationale
- analyse design trade-offs
- justify deliberate departures from common conventions
- ask learners to prioritise competing client/user needs

## English, maths, EDI and employability

**English:** precise labels, concise annotation, technical vocabulary and justification.

**Maths:** proportion, spacing, alignment, grids and relative dimensions.

**EDI:** inclusive layouts, readable typography, contrast and consideration of diverse user needs.

**Employability:** UX/UI design, requirements interpretation, client communication, documentation and peer critique.

## End-of-session diagnostic

Learners are ready to progress when they have:
- a coherent site map
- an evidenced visual direction
- multiple annotated page layouts
- at least one responsive variation
- clear links between decisions and requirements

Carry incomplete or weak design areas into Week 7 before formal review and refinement.
