## Slide 4 – Retrieval
1. What is a client requirement?
2. What is a user requirement?
3. What is a constraint?
4. What makes a success criterion measurable?
5. Why must designs be traceable back to requirements?

## Slide 5 – Design documentation is evidence
Strong design documentation shows:
- what you intend to build
- how the site will be structured
- how users will navigate
- what pages will contain
- how the site will look and feel
- how it will adapt to different devices
- why decisions were made

## Slide 6 – Four useful document types
- **Site map** – structure and relationships between pages
- **Mood board** – visual direction and design language
- **Wireframe** – page layout and content hierarchy
- **Prototype** – increasingly realistic representation of interaction/appearance

## Slide 7 – Do not confuse them
A site map is not a page layout.  
A mood board is not a finished website.  
A wireframe is not just a pretty sketch.  
A prototype should test ideas, not merely decorate them.

## Slide 8 – Site maps
A site map communicates:
- page hierarchy
- parent/child relationships
- navigation depth
- likely user journeys
- overall information architecture

## Slide 9 – Information architecture
Good information architecture helps users answer:
- Where am I?
- What can I do here?
- Where can I go next?
- How do I find important information?

## Slide 10 – Naming pages
Labels should be:
- clear
- predictable
- concise
- meaningful to users

Avoid labels that only make sense to the designer.

## Slide 11 – User journeys
A site map should support realistic journeys.

Example neutral journey:
**Home → Courses → Course details → Apply**

Ask whether important journeys are unnecessarily long or confusing.

## Slide 12 – Activity: site-map design
Create a site map for your Hot Beans Web proposal.

Check:
- all required content has a home
- hierarchy makes sense
- labels are understandable
- key routes are short
- navigation is consistent

## Slide 13 – Peer route test
Give a partner a goal, for example:
“Find the qualifications needed for a trainee developer role.”

Can they predict the route from your site map alone?

## Slide 14 – Mood boards
A mood board should capture visual direction through:
- colour
- typography
- imagery
- iconography
- spacing
- texture
- shape
- tone
- examples of interface style

## Slide 15 – A mood board needs reasoning
For each important visual choice, ask:
- what quality does this communicate?
- why does it suit the audience?
- how could it influence the final site?

## Slide 16 – Colour choices
Colour can support:
- brand identity
- visual hierarchy
- emphasis
- contrast
- readability
- emotional tone

But strong colour choices must also remain accessible.

## Slide 17 – Typography choices
Consider:
- readability
- hierarchy
- consistency
- tone
- size
- weight
- spacing
- device context

Typography should support communication, not just style.

## Slide 18 – Imagery choices
Ask:
- is the imagery relevant?
- does it suit the target audience?
- does it support the purpose?
- is it credible and professional?
- is there a legal route to use it?
- will it work responsively?

## Slide 19 – Activity: visual direction
Build a mood board that communicates a coherent design direction.

Annotate at least:
- colour
- typography
- imagery
- interface style
- intended audience effect

## Slide 20 – Wireframes
Wireframes communicate page structure before visual polish.

Focus on:
- hierarchy
- content placement
- navigation
- calls to action
- forms
- media
- spacing

## Slide 21 – Low fidelity first
Early wireframes can be simple boxes and labels.

This helps you change structure quickly without becoming attached to visual details.

## Slide 22 – Visual hierarchy
Users should quickly understand:
1. what the page is about
2. what is most important
3. what action they can take
4. where supporting information sits

## Slide 23 – Common page components
- header
- navigation
- hero/title area
- main content
- cards/sections
- call-to-action buttons
- forms
- supporting links
- footer

Not every page needs every component.

## Slide 24 – Responsive design
Responsive design means adapting the layout to available screen space and user context.

It does **not** simply mean shrinking everything.

## Slide 25 – What may change on smaller screens?
- columns stack
- navigation changes form
- images resize/crop
- content priority becomes more obvious
- spacing changes
- controls become touch-friendly
- some secondary content may move or collapse

## Slide 26 – Mobile-first thinking
Mobile-first asks:
“What is essential when space is limited?”

Then progressively enhance the experience for larger screens.

## Slide 27 – Desktop-first thinking
Desktop-first may make it easier to explore richer layouts initially, but requires careful reduction for smaller screens.

Neither approach replaces the need for deliberate responsive planning.

## Slide 28 – Wireframing sprint
Produce wireframes for at least three key pages.

Include:
- clear content hierarchy
- navigation
- relevant calls to action
- required content
- at least one responsive/mobile variant

## Slide 29 – Annotation: weak vs strong
Weak:
> “This is the navigation bar.”

Stronger:
> “The primary navigation remains in a consistent position so users can predict where to find key sections, supporting usability for job-seekers moving between vacancies, profiles and course links.”

## Slide 30 – Traceability
Every important design decision should connect to at least one of:
- client requirement
- user need
- purpose
- accessibility consideration
- success criterion

## Slide 31 – Annotation framework
Use:

**Decision → Requirement/User need → Intended effect**

Example neutral structure:
> “I placed ___ because requirement ___ means users need ___. This should help by ___.”

## Slide 32 – Design audit
Check your work:
- Does every required page exist?
- Are user journeys clear?
- Is visual style coherent?
- Are wireframes consistent?
- Have you considered mobile/tablet?
- Are annotations explanatory rather than descriptive?
