# Week 15 Teacher Guidance – Review & Evaluation

## Teaching intent

The main risk in this session is that learners write a retrospective feature list instead of an evaluation. Keep returning to:

> **What is the judgement, and what evidence supports it?**

The evaluation should be built from evidence produced throughout the project rather than reconstructed from memory at the end.

---

## Core progression

Use this repeatedly:

**requirement → evidence → effect → limitation/trade-off → alternative → judgement**

Learners do not need every paragraph to contain every element, but stronger evaluation should show this kind of reasoning across the whole response.

---

## Retrieval indicative answers

1. A planned set of checks defining what is tested, conditions/data and expected outcomes.
2. Expected = prediction before execution; actual = observed outcome.
3. A fault/problem where actual behaviour differs from required/expected behaviour.
4. It preserves authentic evidence of the development process and explains why a change was needed.
5. Repeating a relevant test after a change to see whether the issue is resolved.
6. Checking that a change has not broken previously working functionality.
7. Evidence-led improvement of the solution.
8. Without evidence, the developer cannot demonstrate that the change was needed or successful.
9. Examples: screenshots, test results, file size, performance measurements, user feedback, changed code.
10. Alternatives reveal trade-offs and support justification.
11. Deciding relative importance/urgency based on impact, severity, requirement importance and available effort.
12. It provides an explicit benchmark for fitness for purpose.

Challenge: B is stronger because it reports evidence, scope and a limitation instead of making an unsupported absolute claim.

---

## Modelling evaluation safely

Use a generic scenario rather than Hot Beans-specific conclusions.

A useful model progression:

### Description
> The website has a contact form.

### Explanation
> The contact form lets users send an enquiry.

### Analysis
> Validation reduces incomplete entries and provides immediate guidance.

### Evaluation
> Testing showed empty required fields produced clear feedback and valid inputs passed the client-side checks. This improves recovery from errors, although the demonstration does not include server-side processing, so it would not be sufficient as a complete production solution. For a prototype, the approach is appropriate because it demonstrates the intended user interaction while keeping the scope manageable.

Ask learners to identify evidence, limitation and judgement.

---

## Common weak patterns

### Feature inventory

> “I used HTML, CSS, JavaScript, a form, images and navigation.”

Prompt: *How well did those decisions meet the requirement?*

### Unsupported adjective

> “The site is professional.”

Prompt: *Which specific design decisions and evidence make that judgement defensible?*

### Absolute compatibility claim

> “It works on every browser.”

Prompt: *Which browsers did you actually test?*

### Fake evaluation through negatives

Learners sometimes add trivial weaknesses simply to appear balanced.

Prompt: *What limitation was actually revealed by testing, feedback or reflection?*

### Future feature wish-list

An evaluation should not become a long list of unrelated enhancements.

Prompt: *What evidence shows this future improvement is needed?*

---

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer evidence that is specific and traceable:

1. requirement + test result + retest result;
2. before/after evidence tied to a genuine issue;
3. task-based user feedback;
4. screenshots/code where they demonstrate a relevant decision;
5. reflective claim without direct evidence.

A screenshot alone rarely proves usability, accessibility or performance.

---

## Peer review boundaries

Peer review should identify questions and gaps rather than generate replacement assessment prose.

Useful peer questions:

- Where is the evidence for this claim?
- Which requirement does this link to?
- What limitation remains?
- What alternative was possible?
- What final judgement follows from this evidence?

---

## Adaptive teaching

### Learners struggling with extended writing

Use a six-box planning frame:

1. requirement;
2. evidence;
3. strength;
4. limitation;
5. alternative;
6. judgement.

Have the learner verbalise the chain before writing.

### Learners ready for distinction-level reasoning

Push them toward:

- competing evidence;
- trade-offs;
- quality/limitations of testing evidence;
- justified rejection of alternatives;
- partial rather than binary success;
- effects of working practices on the finished solution.

---

## Useful evaluative vocabulary

Encourage accurate use of:

- effective;
- partly effective;
- appropriate;
- proportionate;
- suitable;
- limitation;
- trade-off;
- consequence;
- evidence indicates;
- testing demonstrated;
- feedback suggested;
- however;
- whereas;
- therefore;
- on balance.

Discourage inflated language where evidence does not support it.

---

## Assessment-safe conferencing

Use questions instead of conclusions:

- Show me the requirement you are evaluating.
- What is your best evidence?
- What does that evidence allow you to conclude?
- What can you not conclude?
- What was the cost/trade-off of your approach?
- Would a different approach have been better? Why?
- Has optimisation fully resolved the issue?

---

## Exit-ticket indicative answers

1. Evaluation makes a supported judgement about effectiveness, not merely a statement of what exists.
2. Examples: task completion, user feedback, navigation tests, form-error recovery, keyboard testing.
3. Requirements provide the benchmark for fitness for purpose.
4. Limitations demonstrate balance and allow the learner to judge effectiveness realistically.
5. A compromise where gaining one benefit may introduce a cost/disadvantage elsewhere.
6. Accept any evidence-based statement that reports tested responsive behaviour and includes a judgement/limitation.
7. Individual response.

---

## Preparation for Week 16

Learners should leave Week 15 with a substantial evaluation plan and at least one evidence-rich drafted section. Week 16 can then focus on distinction-level synthesis, professional practice and final evidence quality rather than teaching evaluation from scratch.
