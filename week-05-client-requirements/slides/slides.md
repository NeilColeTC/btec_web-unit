## Slide 5 – Assignment 2 scenario
Hot Beans Web needs a new recruitment-focused section of its website aimed at prospective web developers.

Your role: junior web developer.

---

## Slide 6 – Required content
The brief requires:
- company profile
- trainee developer profiles
- job specifications and required qualifications
- online application form
- links to web-development courses

---

## Slide 7 – Intended audience
The stated audience is people who:
- have completed relevant web-development training/qualifications
- are looking for work as web developers

Question: what might this audience need from the website?

---

## Slide 8 – Client requirement vs user need
**Client requirement:** what the client asks the product to contain or achieve.

**User need:** what the intended user needs in order to use the product effectively.

They overlap, but they are not identical.

---

## Slide 9 – Explicit requirements
Explicit requirements are directly stated in the brief.

Examples:
- an online application form
- trainee profiles
- course links

These should be evidenced clearly in your design documentation.

---

## Slide 10 – Inferred requirements
Some requirements are reasonable implications rather than direct statements.

Examples might include:
- clear navigation
- readable content
- mobile usability
- accessible form labels

Do not pretend an inference was explicitly stated by the client.

---

## Slide 11 – Requirement classification
Classify each as:
- explicit client requirement
- inferred user requirement
- possible design idea

Why does the distinction matter?

---

## Slide 12 – What is a problem definition?
A problem definition explains:
- who the client/audience are
- what problem must be solved
- what the website needs to achieve
- important constraints
- expected benefits
- required level of interactivity/complexity

---

## Slide 13 – Weak problem definition
> I am going to make a nice website with blue colours and lots of pages.

What is missing?

---

## Slide 14 – Stronger structure
A stronger problem definition answers:
1. What is the current need/problem?
2. Who is the intended audience?
3. What must the solution achieve?
4. What constraints matter?
5. What benefits should result?

---

## Slide 15 – Tutor model
Use a neutral scenario.

Identify:
- purpose
- audience
- explicit requirements
- user needs
- constraints
- benefits

Then observe how those are combined into a concise problem definition.

---

## Slide 16 – Your task
Draft your own Hot Beans Web problem definition.

Do not describe finished colours/layouts yet.

Focus on the problem and requirements.

---

## Slide 17 – Constraints
Constraints limit or shape possible solutions.

Examples:
- time
- available assets
- required content
- browser/device compatibility
- accessibility expectations
- legal requirements

---

## Slide 18 – Important caution
Do not invent constraints that are not supported.

You may identify realistic project/design constraints, but distinguish them from stated client requirements.

---

## Slide 19 – Benefits
A successful solution may benefit:
- the client
- prospective applicants
- existing staff
- recruitment processes

A benefit explains a positive outcome, not merely a feature.

---

## Slide 20 – Feature vs benefit
**Feature:** online application form.

**Benefit:** applicants can submit information directly through the website, making the recruitment process more convenient.

---

## Slide 21 – Interactivity
Interactivity means the user can actively engage with elements of the website.

Examples:
- forms
- menus
- buttons
- validation
- interactive content

Interactivity should serve user needs.

---

## Slide 22 – Complexity
Complexity should be appropriate to purpose.

More code/features does not automatically mean a better solution.

Ask: does the complexity improve fitness for purpose?

---

## Slide 23 – Success criteria
Success criteria define how you will judge whether the final website meets requirements.

Good criteria are:
- specific
- observable
- testable
- linked to requirements

---

## Slide 24 – Weak vs measurable
Weak:
> The website will look good.

Better:
> All required pages will use a consistent navigation structure and visual style.

Weak:
> The form will work.

Better:
> Required fields will be validated and users will receive clear feedback when input is missing or invalid.

---

## Slide 25 – Rewrite challenge
Improve these criteria:
1. The site is easy to use.
2. It works on phones.
3. It is accessible.
4. It has all the right information.

---

## Slide 26 – Requirement-to-evidence mapping
For each requirement, ask:

**What design evidence will show I planned for this?**

Possible evidence:
- site map
- wireframe
- mood board
- page layout
- form design
- asset plan
- testing plan

---

## Slide 27 – Example mapping
Requirement: online application form.

Potential design evidence:
- form wireframe
- planned fields
- validation plan
- accessibility notes
- pseudocode/flowchart for scripted validation
