# Week 5 Teacher Guidance – Client Requirements and Assignment 2

## Curriculum purpose

Week 5 is the bridge between Learning Aim A and Learning Aim B. Learners now stop evaluating somebody else’s website and begin designing a solution to a vocational client brief.

The core progression is:

**read accurately → extract requirements → distinguish needs/ideas → define the problem → create measurable criteria → plan evidence**

## Source-bound requirements

Keep the Assignment 2 scenario central. The brief requires the learner to create a recruitment-focused section for Hot Beans Web containing:

- company profile
- profiles of existing trainee web developers
- job specifications and required qualifications
- online application form
- links to web-development courses

The stated target audience is people who have completed relevant web-development training or qualifications and are now looking for work as web developers.

Do not silently add extra client requirements.

## Specification coverage

B1 requires learners to understand the steps involved in developing a design for a client website, including:

- problem definition
- intended audience
- summary of the problem
- constraints
- benefits
- nature of interactivity
- complexity
- purpose requirements from a client brief
- initial ideas/prototypes
- feedback and refinement
- testing plan requirements
- technical/design constraints

Week 5 focuses on the first analytical and planning elements so that Weeks 6–8 can build robust design evidence.

## Assessment boundary

Assignment 2 is summative. Therefore:

- do not provide a complete Hot Beans Web problem definition
- do not provide a finished requirement list learners can submit unchanged
- do not dictate design decisions such as colour schemes/layouts
- do not turn inferred user needs into falsely stated client requirements
- use neutral scenarios for fully worked models
- provide frameworks, prompts, questioning and feedback

## Suggested tutor modelling

Use a non-assessment scenario, for example a local sports club recruiting volunteers.

Model how to move from a brief to:

1. explicit client requirements
2. intended audience
3. likely user needs
4. constraints
5. benefits
6. interactivity/complexity
7. measurable success criteria

Then remove the model before learners complete their own Hot Beans work.

## Indicative retrieval answers

1. **Website purpose:** what the website is intended to achieve.
2. **Target audience:** the intended group of users.
3. Design principles could include usability, navigation, accessibility, typography, consistency, clarity or layout.
4. Performance factors include bandwidth, file size/type, browser, cache, processor speed, upload/download speed or interactivity.
5. SEO = Search Engine Optimisation.
6. **Client requirement:** something the client asks the website to contain, do or achieve.
7. **User requirement:** something the intended user needs in order to use the website effectively.
8. Client and user needs may differ because the client commissions the product while users interact with it.
9. **Constraint:** something that limits or shapes possible solutions.
10. A useful success criterion is specific, observable/testable and linked to a requirement.

## Guidance for requirements analysis

Encourage learners to distinguish three categories:

- **Explicit:** directly stated in the brief.
- **Inferred:** reasonable user/technical need arising from purpose/audience.
- **Design decision:** a choice the learner may make later.

This distinction is important because weak BTEC evidence often presents design choices as though they were given by the client.

Useful questioning:

- Where exactly does the brief say that?
- Is that a requirement or your proposed solution?
- Who needs this feature and why?
- How would you prove later that this was successful?
- What would happen if this requirement were ignored?

## Problem-definition guidance

A strong problem definition should summarise rather than copy the brief. It should establish:

- the organisational need
- intended users
- required outcomes
- constraints
- benefits
- appropriate interactivity/complexity

Avoid encouraging learners to prematurely specify visual details such as exact colours, fonts or layouts unless these are genuine constraints.

## Success-criteria guidance

Learners will later test and evaluate the site. Week 5 should therefore establish useful criteria now.

Weak:
- “looks good”
- “is easy to use”
- “works on mobile”

Stronger criteria identify observable outcomes, for example consistent navigation, presence of required content, responsive behaviour, clear form feedback or accessible labels.

Do not prescribe exact assessed wording; require learners to construct their own criteria.

## Common misconceptions and interventions

### “The client says the site must be blue.”
Ask learners to locate this in the brief. If absent, classify it as a possible design choice, not a requirement.

### “Responsive design is in the brief.”
Clarify the difference between an explicit requirement and a justified professional/user requirement derived from the specification and expected use.

### “A constraint is something bad.”
Explain that constraints shape the design space and can support realistic professional decision making.

### “Success means the client likes it.”
Client/user feedback matters, but success criteria should also be observable and linked to requirements.

### “More features = higher grade.”
Emphasise fitness for purpose, evidence and justification rather than unnecessary complexity.

## Adaptive teaching

### Support

- colour-coded brief annotation
- structured requirement tables
- sentence stems
- oral rehearsal before writing
- examples of explicit/inferred/design-choice distinctions
- tutor checkpoints before moving to wireframing

### Stretch

- functional vs non-functional requirements
- critical/desirable/optional prioritisation
- requirement conflicts and trade-offs
- measurable accessibility/responsive criteria
- direct links between current success criteria and later C.P5/BC.D2 evidence

## English, maths, EDI and employability

**English:** accurate reading of a vocational brief, summarisation, formal problem-definition writing, technical vocabulary.

**Maths:** measurable success criteria, dimensions, proportions and responsive thresholds where justified.

**EDI:** inclusive audience analysis, accessibility and avoiding stereotypes about applicants.

**Employability:** client communication, requirements engineering, planning, accountability and professional documentation.

## End-of-session diagnostic

Learners are ready for Week 6 when they can show:

- an accurate annotated brief
- a viable problem definition
- a clearly stated intended audience
- separated client and user requirements
- plausible constraints and benefits
- measurable success criteria

Any gaps here should be corrected before significant visual design work starts.
